Built on 10 qualitative interviews with chefs across five countries, the product solves a single operational moment: the gap between order placement and delivery arrival.

The Problem

Not that something
is missing.
Not knowing.

In 10 qualitative interviews with chefs from Switzerland, France, Spain, Germany, and Uruguay, the same pattern appeared every time. Late deliveries, quality problems, quantity discrepancies. Each one discovered at the worst possible moment: when the delivery truck is already at the door and the service starts in two hours.

Sixty percent of chefs surveyed reported direct stress from late supplier deliveries. The rest flagged quality or quantity issues. In every case, the damage was not the problem itself. It was the absence of time to react.

Luxury gastronomy runs on WhatsApp, phone calls, and paper. Nothing is documented. No system warns in advance. The chef absorbs the consequences even when the mistake originated with the supplier.

At that moment, the service is already at risk. There is no time left to react.

Process & Key Decisions

Three decisions that
shaped the product

Each decision was a correction. A moment where the design process forced a choice between what looked good and what the research actually supported.

01 — Back to the User
Scope Before Features
During competitive analysis, products like Apicbase and Haddock created scope drift. AI photo recognition, automatic complaints, complex supplier portals. When defining the MVP, the design had nothing to do with what chefs described in interviews. The correction was clear: Supply Pro solves one single moment. The moment between the order and the delivery.
02 — The Niche Defines the Design
Domain Knowledge as Design Input
Kitchens under time pressure and high stress do not need an impressive interface. They need one that works. This shaped every design decision: no decorative elements, no complex flows, no hidden actions. Functional beats decorative. Seventeen years as a head pastry chef and cook in Michelin-starred restaurants was not a nice addition. It was the decisive advantage.
03 — UX Writing Is Not a Detail
Language as Interface
The first usability test showed that several terms were unclear or contradictory. Testers hesitated not because the interface was poorly structured, but because they were unsure what an action meant. A chef does not read, they scan. This forced a complete overhaul of the UX writing and visual hierarchy. The F-pattern layout stayed. The language was rebuilt from scratch.
01

Dashboard — Order Overview. The dashboard shows what is happening today at a glance. Open orders, confirmed deliveries, active alerts. The information architecture follows the F-pattern: the most critical information top left, status information to the right. A chef has three seconds. Everything must be scannable without reading.

02

Early Warning — Alert Screen. The core of Supply Pro. When a supplier reports a problem, the warning appears proactively before the chef has to look for it. The screen shows which product is affected, what the problem is, and what options are available. The chef confirms. They do not decide from nothing.

03

Alternative Product Selection. When a product is unavailable, the system automatically suggests a replacement. Price, delivery time, and availability visible at a glance. The chef chooses, the system sends. No phone call, no WhatsApp, no manual documentation.

The Solution

Three screens.
One clear flow.

Supply Pro focuses on a single operational moment: the gap between placing an order and receiving a delivery. Three screens, one unambiguous flow.

The product does not replace existing kitchen management tools. It fills the blind spot they all share: the silence between the order and the arrival.

From the first tap to a confirmed decision in under three minutes.

Testing & Iteration

What the test
actually revealed

01
What Was Tested
The test goal was clear: is the early warning system understandable and easy to use for chefs? Four people from the gastronomy environment tested the prototype. The scenario was realistic: two simultaneous problem alerts — a quality issue and a delay — to be resolved within three minutes.
02
What Worked
The F-pattern layout held. Testers scanned information intuitively from top left to right. No tester had difficulty locating the relevant information. The visual hierarchy guided attention without instruction.
03
What Failed
The UX writing. Several terms were unclear or contradictory. Testers hesitated not because the interface was poorly structured, but because they were unsure what an action meant. That is a different problem with significant consequences.
04
What Changed
Complete overhaul of the UX writing. Every term was reviewed: is it unambiguous? Does it trigger the right expectation? The typographic scale was adjusted and a clearer visual hierarchy through color was introduced. The layout stayed. The language was rebuilt.

AI in the Design Process

One tool per
phase of the process

AI was not a single tool. It was a system of specialized tools — one matched to each phase of the Design Thinking process.

01 — Research & Competitive Analysis
Perplexity
Fast, source-based benchmark and competitive research. Used to analyze Apicbase, Haddock, and the broader market landscape before the first interview.
02 — Interviews & Documentation
Granola + Claude
Granola for transcription of qualitative interviews. Claude for synthesizing insights, clustering findings, and publishing documentation in Notion. Ten interviews processed without a single manual transcript.
03 — UX Writing & Microcopy
Claude
Testing different tones and terms across status badges and alert texts in Spanish and German. The language iteration that saved the product after usability testing was built entirely in Claude.
04 — Wireframes & Prototype
Miro + Google Stitch + Figma Make
Miro for lo-fi wireframes. Google Stitch and Figma Make for the mid-fi prototype. Each tool was evaluated against one criterion: which one reaches the goal fastest?
05 — Deployment
Claude Code + GitHub + Vercel
Claude Code for building the landing page. GitHub as repository. Vercel for deployment. Supply Pro is live. The full product development cycle completed without a dedicated engineering team.
Lessons Learned

What this project
taught me

01
Tight Scope Is a Strategic Decision
The decision to focus on one single moment saved the product. A tight scope is not a weakness. It is the only way to build an honest MVP that delivers real value without overcomplicating execution.
02
Domain Knowledge Sharpens Methods
Seventeen years in professional kitchens was not a nice addition to the research. It was the reason the interviews went deep and the design decisions held. Domain knowledge does not replace UX methods. It sharpens them.
03
Test with Lo-Fi Earlier
The first usability test came too late in the process. Two iteration rounds could have been saved. Low-fidelity testing surfaces language and flow problems before visual decisions lock them in.
04
Include All Stakeholders Earlier
An interview with a supplier revealed that the supplier side has its own pain points the product needs to address. Anticipation is necessary for both parties. The supplier also benefits from communicating problems before they escalate.
